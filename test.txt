Yes. What you are describing is a **stacked / two-stage model**:

$$
\text{Bureau features}\rightarrow XGB_B\rightarrow PD_B
$$

$$
\text{Transaction features}\rightarrow XGB_T\rightarrow PD_T
$$

and then:

$$
(PD_B,PD_T)\rightarrow Logistic Regression\rightarrow PD_{final}
$$

This is a good architecture when you want the final combination to remain interpretable.

Suppose your meta-model is:

$$
\operatorname{logit}(PD_{final})
=
\beta_0+
\beta_B z_B+
\beta_T z_T
$$

where \(z_B\) and \(z_T\) are the outputs from the two XGBoost models.

### 1. First decide what to feed into logistic regression

You could use the raw probabilities:

$$
\operatorname{logit}(PD_{final})
=
\beta_0+\beta_B PD_B+\beta_T PD_T
$$

but I would usually consider using the **log-odds** of the two model predictions:

$$
z_B = \log\frac{PD_B}{1-PD_B}
$$

$$
z_T = \log\frac{PD_T}{1-PD_T}
$$

Then:

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{logit}(PD_{final})
=
\beta_0+
\beta_B\operatorname{logit}(PD_B)
+
\beta_T\operatorname{logit}(PD_T)
}
$$

This tends to be more natural because your XGBoost classifiers and final logistic regression are operating on a compatible score scale.

For example, suppose:

$$
PD_B = 20\%, \qquad PD_T=10\%
$$

Then:

$$
\operatorname{logit}(0.20)=-1.386
$$

$$
\operatorname{logit}(0.10)=-2.197
$$

Suppose your meta-model is:

$$
\text{Final logit}
=
0.2
+
0.7z_B
+
0.4z_T
$$

Then:

$$
=0.2+0.7(-1.386)+0.4(-2.197)
$$

$$
=-1.649
$$

and therefore:

$$
PD_{final}=\sigma(-1.649)\approx16.1\%
$$

So your final model says this borrower has approximately **16.1% PD**.

---

### 2. Explaining the final prediction becomes very clean

At the **model-combination level**, you can explain:

$$
\underbrace{\beta_0}_{baseline}
+
\underbrace{\beta_Bz_B}_{bureau\ contribution}
+
\underbrace{\beta_Tz_T}_{transaction\ contribution}
$$

For one borrower, you might get:

| Component         | Contribution to final log-odds |
| ----------------- | -----------------------------: |
| Intercept         |                          +0.20 |
| Bureau model      |                          -0.97 |
| Transaction model |                          -0.88 |
| Final score       |                          -1.65 |
| Final PD          |                      **16.1%** |

This answers the first business question:

> **How much did the bureau model versus transaction model contribute to this final risk assessment?**

But usually you will want to go one level deeper.

---

### 3. Explain each XGBoost model separately with SHAP

Suppose the bureau XGBoost prediction is:

$$
\operatorname{logit}(PD_B)
=
Base_B+\sum_j SHAP_{B,j}
$$

and the transaction model is:

$$
\operatorname{logit}(PD_T)
=
Base_T+\sum_k SHAP_{T,k}
$$

Now substitute these into the final logistic regression:

$$
\operatorname{logit}(PD_{final})
=
\beta_0+
\beta_B
\left(
Base_B+\sum_j SHAP_{B,j}
\right)
+
\beta_T
\left(
Base_T+\sum_k SHAP_{T,k}
\right)
$$

Expand it:

$$
=
\beta_0
+\beta_B Base_B
+\beta_T Base_T
+\sum_j\beta_B SHAP_{B,j}
+\sum_k\beta_T SHAP_{T,k}
$$

And this gives you something very useful.

For each **original bureau feature**:

$$
\boxed{
Contribution_j=\beta_B\times SHAP_{B,j}
}
$$

For each **original transaction feature**:

$$
\boxed{
Contribution_k=\beta_T\times SHAP_{T,k}
}
$$

So you can trace the final model prediction all the way back to the original variables.

---

### A concrete credit-risk example

Imagine the Bureau XGBoost SHAP explanation is:

| Bureau feature        |  SHAP |
| --------------------- | ----: |
| Past due count        | +0.70 |
| Bureau score          | -0.50 |
| Credit utilization    | +0.30 |
| Credit history length | -0.20 |

and:

$$
\beta_B=0.7
$$

Then their contributions to the **final stacked model** become:

| Feature        | XGB SHAP | Meta coefficient | Final contribution |
| -------------- | -------: | ---------------: | -----------------: |
| Past due count |    +0.70 |             0.70 |          **+0.49** |
| Bureau score   |    -0.50 |             0.70 |          **-0.35** |
| Utilization    |    +0.30 |             0.70 |          **+0.21** |
| History length |    -0.20 |             0.70 |          **-0.14** |

Now suppose transaction SHAP values are:

| Transaction feature   |  SHAP |
| --------------------- | ----: |
| Gambling transactions | +0.80 |
| Salary stability      | -0.60 |
| Overdraft frequency   | +0.50 |

with:

$$
\beta_T=0.4
$$

Their final contributions are:

| Feature               | XGB SHAP | Meta coefficient | Final contribution |
| --------------------- | -------: | ---------------: | -----------------: |
| Gambling transactions |    +0.80 |             0.40 |          **+0.32** |
| Salary stability      |    -0.60 |             0.40 |          **-0.24** |
| Overdraft frequency   |    +0.50 |             0.40 |          **+0.20** |

You can therefore explain the final decision as:

> The final PD is 16.1%. The main factors increasing risk were past-due history, gambling-related transactions, high utilization, and frequent overdrafts. Stable salary flows, a strong bureau score, and longer credit history reduced the estimated risk.

That is much more interpretable than treating the ensemble as a black box.

### One important training detail

There is a major mistake to avoid when building this.

Don't train your XGBoost models on the entire training dataset and then take their **in-sample predictions** to train the logistic regression. The second-stage model will receive overly optimistic predictions.

Instead use **out-of-fold predictions**:

```text
Training data
     │
     ├── Bureau XGB ──────────────┐
     │    OOF predictions         │
     │                            ├── Logistic meta-model
     └── Transaction XGB ─────────┤
          OOF predictions         │
                                  ↓
                              Final PD
```

For example, with 5-fold CV:

1. Train Bureau XGB on folds 1–4 → predict fold 5.
2. Train Transaction XGB on folds 1–4 → predict fold 5.
3. Repeat until every observation has an out-of-fold prediction.
4. You now have `bureau_oof_pd` and `transaction_oof_pd`.
5. Train logistic regression on those two columns.
6. Retrain the two XGBoost models on all development data for production.

This prevents **stacking leakage**.

One final point: the simple `β × SHAP` decomposition is cleanest when the meta-model consumes the XGBoost **raw/logit outputs**, because SHAP's additive decomposition is normally on that raw scale. If your meta-model consumes probabilities instead, the sigmoid between XGBoost SHAP and the meta-model introduces non-linearity, so you should not blindly multiply probability-space SHAP values by the logistic coefficient.

For a credit-risk setup where explainability matters, I'd favor:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Original features}
\rightarrow
\text{XGBoost raw/logit scores}
\rightarrow
\text{Logistic regression}
\rightarrow
PD
}
$$

It gives you both **model-level attribution** (bureau vs transaction) and **feature-level attribution** (which bureau/transaction variables drove the final PD).
